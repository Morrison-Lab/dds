Definition 1 (Tensor)

Definition 1 (Tensor) A tensor is an array of numbers arranged on a regular grid with any number of dimensions [3, Sec. 2.1]. Every entry is picked out by one index per dimension.

A tensor with no dimensions is a scalar, one with one dimension is a vector, and one with two is a matrix. PyTorch says “dimensions” (ndim, and dim= in its functions), where NumPy, which also says ndim, calls them “axes” (axis=); other sources say “modes”, or call the number of dimensions the tensor’s “order” or “rank”. That “rank” is not the rank of a matrix. Physics and mathematics use “tensor” for a multilinear map, which an array of numbers represents once a basis is chosen; deep learning means only the array.

Definition 4 (Broadcasting)

Definition 4 (Broadcasting) Broadcasting is how an elementwise operation such as +, -, * or / combines two tensors of different shapes [3, Sec. 2.1]. Line the two shapes up from their last dimension backwards, and treat a dimension that one tensor lacks at the front as having size 1. The shapes are compatible when, in every dimension, the two sizes are equal or one of them is 1. In each dimension, the result takes the size that is not 1, or the common size when the two are equal. A tensor with size 1 in a dimension uses its one entry along the whole of it. When the shapes are not compatible, PyTorch raises an error. PyTorch’s broadcasting semantics page states the same rule, which PyTorch takes from NumPy.

3 Automatic differentiation

3.1 The soft 0-or-1 rule

The rest of this page differentiates the soft 0-or-1 rule of the machine learning review. Among the images of 0s and 1s, it gives each image the probability \(\hprob = \sigmoidf{\predvar - \param}\) of being a 1, where \(\predvar\) is the image’s center ink, its average pixel over rows 2 to 5 and columns 3 and 4, and \(\param\) is the rule’s cutoff. Its mean log loss over those \(\nobs\) images, its empirical risk under that loss, is

\[ \empriskf{\param} = -\avgin \sb{\outvar_i \log \hprob_i + (1 - \outvar_i) \log (1 - \hprob_i)}, \]

where \(\outvar_i\) is 1 if image \(i\) is a 1 and 0 if it is a 0.

3.2 The slope by hand

Exercise 11 (The slope of the log loss)

Exercise 11 (The slope of the log loss) Find the derivative \(\dif{\emprisk}{\param}\) of the mean log loss with respect to the cutoff.

Hint

The logistic function has derivative \(\sigmoid'(z) = \sigmoidf{z}\paren{1 - \sigmoidf{z}}\) (lds).

Solution 11

Solution 11. Write \(\obsloss{i}\) for image \(i\)’s log loss, \(\obsloss{i} = -\outvar_i \log \hprob_i - (1 - \outvar_i) \log (1 - \hprob_i)\). Its derivative with respect to \(\hprob_i\) is

\[ \dif{\obsloss{i}}{\hprob_i} = -\frac{\outvar_i}{\hprob_i} + \frac{1 - \outvar_i}{1 - \hprob_i}. \]

Since \(\hprob_i = \sigmoidf{\predvar_i - \param}\), the chain rule and the derivative of the logistic function give

\[ \begin{aligned} \dif{\hprob_i}{\param} &= \sigmoid'(\predvar_i - \param) \cdot \dif{}{\param} (\predvar_i - \param) \\ &= \hprob_i (1 - \hprob_i) \cdot (-1) \\ &= -\hprob_i (1 - \hprob_i). \end{aligned} \]

Multiplying the two,

\[ \begin{aligned} \dif{\obsloss{i}}{\param} &= \dif{\obsloss{i}}{\hprob_i} \cdot \dif{\hprob_i}{\param} \\ &= \paren{-\frac{\outvar_i}{\hprob_i} + \frac{1 - \outvar_i}{1 - \hprob_i}} \cdot \paren{-\hprob_i (1 - \hprob_i)} \\ &= \outvar_i (1 - \hprob_i) - (1 - \outvar_i) \hprob_i \\ &= \outvar_i - \outvar_i \hprob_i - \hprob_i + \outvar_i \hprob_i \\ &= \outvar_i - \hprob_i. \end{aligned} \]

\(\emprisk\) is the mean of the \(\obsloss{i}\), so its derivative is the mean of theirs:

\[ \dif{\emprisk}{\param} = \avgin (\outvar_i - \hprob_i). \]

Theorem 1 (The slope of the soft rule’s log loss)

Theorem 1 (The slope of the soft rule’s log loss) The mean log loss \(\emprisk\) of the soft 0-or-1 rule has derivative

\[ \dif{\emprisk}{\param} = \avgin (\outvar_i - \hprob_i) \]

with respect to its cutoff \(\param\).

Proof

Proof. By Solution 11. The rule is a logistic regression whose coefficient on \(\predvar\) is fixed at 1 and whose intercept is \(-\param\), so the formula is also the negative of the intercept component of lds’s gradient of the log loss, divided by \(\nobs\) to give the mean.

By Theorem 1, the slope is the share of 1s among the images minus the average probability the rule gives them. The 1s make up a share of 0.506 of the 360 images, and at \(\param = 5\) the rule’s average probability of a 1 is 0.547. So the slope at \(\param = 5\) is -0.0418. It is negative, so raising the cutoff from 5 lowers the log loss. The slope is 0 at the cutoff where the average probability equals the share of 1s.

3.3 The slope by autograd

Exercise 12 (Asking PyTorch for the slope)

Exercise 12 (Asking PyTorch for the slope) Write a function that computes \(\empriskf{\param}\) with PyTorch operations. Make \(\param = 5\) a tensor created with requires_grad=True, compute \(\empriskf{5}\), and pass both to torch.autograd.grad.

Solution 12

Solution 12.

Show code
def mean_log_loss(theta):
    """Mean log loss of the soft 0-or-1 rule with cutoff `theta`."""
    p = torch.sigmoid(ink_01 - theta)
    return -torch.mean(is_one * torch.log(p) + (1 - is_one) * torch.log(1 - p))

theta = torch.tensor(5.0, dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)
loss = mean_log_loss(theta)
(slope_by_autograd,) = torch.autograd.grad(loss, theta)
print(loss)
tensor(0.0853, dtype=torch.float64, grad_fn=<NegBackward0>)
Show code
print(slope_by_autograd)
tensor(-0.0418, dtype=torch.float64)

torch.autograd.grad returns -0.0418. The code for \(\emprisk\) says nothing about its derivative. Because theta was made with requires_grad=True, PyTorch recorded each operation that turned it into loss (the subtraction ink_01 - theta, the logistic function, and each operation after them, down to the mean and its change of sign), and torch.autograd.grad applied the chain rule to those operations, one at a time. The grad_fn that loss prints is the record of its last operation.

Definition 6 (Automatic differentiation (autodiff))

Definition 6 (Automatic differentiation (autodiff)) Automatic differentiation, also called autodiff, computes the derivatives of a function that is given as code, by applying the chain rule to the elementary operations the code performs as it runs [1], [3, Sec. 6.5].

PyTorch implements it in its torch.autograd module. The result is the exact derivative of the operations the code performs, up to floating-point rounding. Automatic differentiation is neither symbolic differentiation, which manipulates a formula for the function, nor numerical differentiation, which approximates a derivative from the function’s values at nearby points. lds’s backpropagation chapter derives by hand the derivatives that autodiff computes for a neural network.

Example 7 (The cutoff’s slope by autodiff)

Example 7 (The cutoff’s slope by autodiff) Solution 12 is automatic differentiation: PyTorch was given only the code for \(\emprisk\), and returned its derivative at \(\param = 5\), which matches the formula of Theorem 1 to within 2.1e-17, as Solution 13 found.

3.4 Gradient descent with autograd

Exercise 14 (Finding the cutoff by gradient descent)

Exercise 14 (Finding the cutoff by gradient descent) Run 100 steps of gradient descent on \(\emprisk\), starting from \(\param = 0\) with learning rate \(\learnrate = 4\), with each step’s derivative from torch.autograd.grad. Where does \(\param\) end up?

Solution 14

Solution 14.

Show code
learning_rate = 4.0
theta = torch.tensor(0.0, dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)
for step in range(100):
    (slope,) = torch.autograd.grad(mean_log_loss(theta), theta)
    with torch.no_grad():
        theta -= learning_rate * slope
theta_descent = theta.item()
(final_slope,) = torch.autograd.grad(mean_log_loss(theta), theta)
print(theta_descent, final_slope.item())
6.59960743783184 -6.833215346312323e-06

Gradient descent ends at \(\param\) = 6.5996, where the slope is -6.8e-06.

The update runs inside torch.no_grad(), which tells PyTorch not to record it: the step is a change to \(\param\), not part of the loss. Without it, PyTorch would record the in-place subtraction on theta, and it raises an error for a recorded in-place change to a tensor that was created with requires_grad=True.

4 Training a model

4.1 The soft rule as a PyTorch model

Exercise 16 (The soft rule as a PyTorch model)

Exercise 16 (The soft rule as a PyTorch model) Write the soft 0-or-1 rule as a subclass of PyTorch’s nn.Module whose cutoff is an nn.Parameter that starts at 0.

Solution 16

Solution 16.

Show code
from torch import nn


class SoftRule(nn.Module):
    """The soft 0-or-1 rule, with its cutoff as a parameter."""

    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.cutoff = nn.Parameter(torch.tensor(0.0, dtype=torch.float64))

    def forward(self, ink):
        return torch.sigmoid(ink - self.cutoff)


rule = SoftRule()
print(list(rule.parameters()))
[Parameter containing:
tensor(0., dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)]
  • __init__ runs nn.Module’s own setup, then creates the cutoff. Assigning an nn.Parameter, a wrapped tensor, to an attribute of the module registers it, so rule.parameters() returns it, as the printout shows. An nn.Parameter is created with requires_grad=True.
  • forward computes the probability of a 1 for each image’s center ink. Calling the module, as rule(ink_01), runs forward.

The trained SoftRule has its cutoff at 6.5996, where the loop of Solution 14 ended, exactly. The code splits that loop into the parts that torch.optim expects:

  • nn.functional.binary_cross_entropy computes the mean log loss, \(\emprisk\), from the probabilities and the 0-or-1 labels.
  • optimizer.zero_grad() clears the gradient stored from the previous step.
  • loss.backward() computes the derivative of the loss with respect to the cutoff, as torch.autograd.grad did, but stores it in the cutoff’s .grad instead of returning it.
  • optimizer.step() moves the cutoff by minus the learning rate times its .grad, the update that Solution 14 ran inside torch.no_grad().

Definition 7 (Training step)

Definition 7 (Training step) A training step in PyTorch updates a model’s parameters once, in four stages:

  1. optimizer.zero_grad() clears the gradient stored in each parameter’s .grad;
  2. calling the model, and then the loss function, computes the loss on a batch of data;
  3. loss.backward() computes the gradient of the loss with respect to each parameter, by automatic differentiation, and adds it to that parameter’s .grad;
  4. optimizer.step() updates each parameter using its .grad, by the rule of the optimizer, such as gradient descent for torch.optim.SGD.

Training repeats the step, over the whole training set or over one batch of it at a time.

Solution 18.

stale_rule = SoftRule()

The cutoff ends at 13.7150, not at 6.5996. loss.backward() adds each new gradient to the one already in .grad. So without optimizer.zero_grad(), the gradient used at each step is the sum of the gradients of every step so far. The first step is the same as in Example 8, with .grad at -0.3759. At the second step, .grad is -0.6199, the first step’s gradient plus the second’s. At 26 of the 100 steps, the cutoff was already past 6.5996, the lowest point of \(\emprisk\), and the summed gradient was still negative, so the step moved the cutoff further past it. The cutoff overshoots and swings back. Over the 100 steps, it ranges from -0.20 to 14.86 and crosses 6.5996 13 times. PyTorch adds rather than replaces so that a gradient can be built up over several backward passes, for example over pieces of a batch too large to fit in memory at once.
